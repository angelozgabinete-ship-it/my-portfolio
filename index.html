Feature · Memoir

A Foundation Day to Remember

CRACK.

Everyone went silent. Arkin looked down. Then he looked at us. For a second, nobody moved. The silence lasted just long enough for all of us to realize what had happened. Then we burst out laughing.

It was probably the last thing any of us expected to happen that morning. We had only just gotten into the van, and we were supposed to be heading to our school’s 38th founding anniversary at the Muntinlupa Sports Complex. But before we could even leave, Arkin had already given us a reason to remember that morning.

✦ ✦ ✦

A few minutes earlier, we had been standing around at Maksim’s house, checking our things and waiting for one person. Arkin. We called him. The phone rang. Again. And again. Finally, he answered.

“Kakagising ko lang po.”

His voice was so groggy and calm that we immediately knew he had just woken up. Everyone else had been ready, while he was probably still figuring out where he was. After gathering his things, he sprinted toward Maksim’s van. His hair was messy, and he still looked half-asleep as he ran toward us. We couldn’t help laughing. He finally reached the van, climbed inside, and sat down.

That was when we heard the crack. And then, somehow, Dylan’s pants ripped too. This time, the laughter came with a little more panic. Two costumes were damaged, and we hadn’t even left yet.

We called Coach Meng and told him what happened. He said he would repair the pants once we arrived. There wasn’t much else we could do, so we checked our things one more time and left. The jokes continued in the van, but as the buildings outside the window changed, my attention slowly shifted toward what was waiting for us. The Muntinlupa Sports Complex. Our Foundation Day. Our performance.

✦ ✦ ✦

When we arrived, Coach Meng immediately worked on the damaged costumes. I stood nearby and looked around the sports complex. There were students everywhere. Groups gathered together in their costumes. Teachers moved around the venue. Voices came from different directions, mixing with music and announcements. The crowd seemed much larger now that I was standing in it.

I looked toward the stage. At Maksim’s house, the performance had felt distant. It was easy to laugh when we were still getting ready. Even the ripped costumes had been funny for a while. Now I could see where we were supposed to perform. The stage didn’t look quite as harmless anymore.

Foundation Day started. One group went up. Then another. I watched them carefully, following their movements even when I already knew I wasn’t performing their routine. Every time a group finished, another one seemed to take its place.

I began going through our dance in my head. One, two, three, four. Then the next set of movements. I stopped. I couldn’t remember one part. I started again. One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight. I looked toward the stage. Another group was performing. The music filled the sports complex, and I watched their movements until the performance ended.

Our turn was getting closer. I tried not to think about it. That made me think about it even more. What if I forgot the steps? What if I made a mistake? What if I looked at the wrong person? What if everyone noticed? Those thoughts kept piling up in my head. The more I tried to make sure I wouldn’t make a mistake, the more nervous I became.

I had always been like that whenever something mattered to me. With tests, presentations, group projects, conversations, or even simple decisions, I could spend too much time thinking about what might go wrong. Sometimes I would imagine the mistake before I even had the chance to make it.

Part of the reason was that I didn’t see mistakes as just mistakes. In my mind, making one could feel like proof that I was incompetent, stupid, or weak. If I forgot something I should have remembered, I would wonder why I couldn’t get something so simple right. If I made a mistake in front of other people, I would imagine them noticing it and thinking less of me. The actual mistake might last only a few seconds, but I could replay it in my head much longer.

That was why the performance felt threatening. I wasn’t only afraid of forgetting a step. I was afraid of what forgetting that step would mean.

I ran through the routine again. The first few counts. The transitions. The ending. Then I started over. Another group finished. The space between their performance and ours was getting smaller. Soon, there would be no more groups to watch. No more time to rehearse the dance in my head. No more waiting.

Coach Meng came over while we were getting ready.

“Wag kayo kabahan, ang importante mag-enjoy kayong lahat.”

It was simple advice, but I remember how difficult it felt to follow. I was still thinking about every step I could get wrong while he was reminding us to enjoy the moment. Looking back, there was something almost funny about it. I was trying to control every possible outcome while he was telling us that the important thing was simply to enjoy ourselves.

✦ ✦ ✦

Our group was called. I looked toward the stage. For a moment, the sounds around me seemed to blur together. I could hear my own breathing. My heart was beating faster, and the lights seemed brighter than they had from the crowd. I could feel the nervousness in my body as I took my place.

The music started. I followed the first movement. Then the next. The steps I had been repeating in my head finally had somewhere to go. I didn’t think about the people watching. I didn’t think about what would happen if I messed up. I just followed the music. One count. Another. Then another. The routine kept moving, and so did we.

The stage looked different from up there. From the crowd, it had seemed enormous. Standing on it, there wasn’t much time to think about its size. I could feel the floor beneath my feet as I moved through the routine, but there was no time to question whether I was doing everything perfectly. There was only the music. And the next step.

Then the last part came. I followed the final movements. The music stopped. For a moment, I just stood there. Then we moved off the stage. I looked back at the crowd. The same place that had seemed so overwhelming when we first arrived was still there. The same students, the same teachers, the same stage. But something about it felt different. Maybe it was because I was no longer waiting.

✦ ✦ ✦

On the way home, I kept thinking about how the day had started. Arkin had overslept. His pants had ripped the moment he sat down. Then Dylan’s did too. We had laughed, panicked, called Coach Meng, and kept going. At the sports complex, I had spent the next few hours doing almost the same thing in my head, imagining every possible way the performance could fall apart before it had even begun.

The funny thing was, I had been afraid of making one mistake on stage when the morning had already given us plenty of them. Maybe I had been looking at mistakes the wrong way. Arkin being late hadn’t stopped us from leaving. The ripped costumes hadn’t stopped us from performing. Standing backstage with my thoughts racing hadn’t stopped me from remembering the first step when the music started. None of those moments disappeared. They just became part of the story.

For a long time, I had treated mistakes as if they were signs that something was wrong with me. If I didn’t do something correctly, I felt like I had failed at more than just that one thing. I could turn a small mistake into a bigger judgment about myself before anyone else even had the chance to react. I often thought through every possible mistake because it felt safer to prepare for failure than to be surprised by it. I thought that if I worried enough, I could somehow prevent things from going wrong.

But that morning showed me that worrying about every possible mistake didn’t actually protect me from anything. The things that went wrong were not the things I had spent hours imagining. Arkin was late. The costumes ripped. We dealt with them. Then we performed. I realized that I had been giving mistakes more power than they deserved. I had been treating them as final judgments when they were usually just moments that needed to be handled.

I don’t think Foundation Day completely changed me. I still overthink things, especially when I care about the result. I still sometimes imagine what could go wrong, and I still worry about disappointing people or looking like I don’t know what I’m doing. But now I try to respond to that feeling differently. When I catch myself thinking too far ahead, I try to focus on the next thing I can actually do instead of every possible thing that could go wrong. If I make a mistake, I try to correct it before judging myself for it. And when I face something uncertain, I remind myself that I don’t have to be completely sure of the outcome before I begin.

I don’t always succeed. But I think that is part of the point. I used to see mistakes as signs of incompetence, stupidity, or weakness. Now, I am starting to see them as part of growing, improving, and learning. A mistake can still be embarrassing or frustrating, but it doesn’t have to decide what happens next.

I think that’s why I still remember the sound of those pants ripping. At the time, it felt like another problem we had to fix. Now, it makes me remember everything that came after it—the laughter, the nervousness, the stage, and the moment when the music finally stopped.

The day didn’t become memorable because everything went according to plan. It became memorable because it didn’t.

Editor’s note: I remember the funny parts first, the late friend and the ripped pants. But the part that really stayed with me was how nervous I got before going on stage.

Special Report · Journalistic Essay

The Greek Freak

By Angelo, Staff Writer

Before the sold-out arenas and the championship parades, there was Athens. There, Giannis Antetokounmpo remembers walking the streets with his brothers, selling whatever they could to help their family. In a 60 Minutes interview with Steve Kroft, Giannis was asked what he used to sell. “Glasses, watches,” he answered. “Then, we used to sell CDs, DVDs.” Kroft asked whether he had been a good salesman. “I was the best,” Giannis replied.

The exchange is lighthearted, almost playful. Giannis smiles at a memory that, beneath the humor, came from necessity. His parents had immigrated from Nigeria to Greece, where the family struggled financially. Giannis and his brothers helped their parents by selling goods on the streets of Athens. At one point, according to the 60 Minutes report, Giannis and his brother Thanasis even shared one pair of basketball shoes during games: one brother would leave the court and hand the shoes to the other.

It is tempting to look at Giannis's childhood only as the beginning of a success story. The poor kid from Athens becomes an NBA superstar. The street vendor becomes a millionaire athlete. The boy selling watches becomes the player whose name fills arenas. But that version leaves out a more complicated question: Where did Giannis belong?

Giannis was born in Athens in 1994 to Nigerian immigrant parents. For much of his childhood, his family did not have the security that citizenship could provide. Giannis and his older brother Thanasis eventually received Greek citizenship in 2013, the same year Giannis entered the NBA. His mother, Veronica, and younger brother Alex did not receive Greek citizenship until 2021. For Giannis, therefore, home was never simply a matter of paperwork. In the 60 Minutes interview, he describes his childhood family life with surprising warmth. His family did not have much money, he explains, but they had happiness. They were together, sometimes all in the same room, and they found ways to enjoy themselves despite the difficulty around them.

That memory matters because belonging often begins before a person has the language to explain it. It can exist in a crowded room, between siblings sharing shoes, or in the comfort of knowing that the people around you will remain when everything else is uncertain.

Basketball eventually became another place where Giannis could belong. His father encouraged Giannis and Thanasis to pursue the sport, and the brothers began playing basketball in Athens. The game gave Giannis something beyond a possible career. It gave him a community and a way to be recognized for something he could do. 60 Minutes describes basketball as helping the brothers fit into their surroundings as they developed as players.

Then, at 18, Giannis left Greece. The Milwaukee Bucks selected him with the 15th pick in the 2013 NBA Draft. He moved from Athens to Milwaukee, entering an unfamiliar country and a professional league that was far larger than the basketball world he had known in Greece.

His arrival in Milwaukee did not erase the question of belonging. It simply moved the question somewhere else. He had come from Greece, but his parents were Nigerian. He was entering American basketball while still carrying the culture and memories of his childhood. Even his eventual nickname, “the Greek Freak,” emphasized one part of his identity while leaving another part unspoken.

Years later, Giannis would explain that this had not always been easy. In a 2022 ESPN interview, he talked about growing up hearing conflicting messages about who he was. Some people in Greece told him he was not Greek enough because his parents were Nigerian and because he did not have the same skin color. But when he spent time around Nigerians in Athens, he sometimes encountered the opposite judgment: he was told he was not Nigerian enough because he did not speak the language fluently. He described himself differently. “I feel Greco-Nigeriano,” he said, explaining that he considers himself both Greek and Nigerian.

That sentence captures the difficulty of belonging to more than one place. People often want identity to be simple. Where are you from? What are you? Which side are you on? But Giannis's experience resists those neat categories. Greece shaped his childhood. Nigeria shaped his family. Milwaukee became the place where his adult life and NBA career developed. Instead of choosing one, he carried all three.

By 2020, Milwaukee was no longer simply the American city where Giannis played basketball. When he signed a long-term extension with the Bucks, he explained why he wanted to remain there. “It’s the place I want to be,” he said. “It’s the place I want to raise my kids. I feel good here. My family feels good here, so I’m good.”

His answer is striking because it shows that belonging can be created as much as inherited. Athens was where Giannis was born. It was where his parents built a life after leaving Nigeria. It was where he and his brothers sold goods on the streets and learned basketball. It was the city that shaped his earliest understanding of family and identity.

But Milwaukee became home, too. That does not mean one place replaced another. In 2021, after Giannis's mother and younger brother received Greek citizenship, Giannis stood with his family at the citizenship ceremony in Athens. He told reporters, “We always felt Greek, but now we have an official stamp and we are happy.”

The phrase “official stamp” is almost startling after everything his family had experienced. For years, belonging had been something the family felt before it was something a government officially recognized. Giannis could feel Greek while still being questioned about whether he was Greek enough. His family could build a life in Athens while living with uncertainty about their legal status. The citizenship did not create the family's connection to Greece. It formalized something that Giannis said they already felt.

That distinction is important. Belonging is not always granted by a document, a jersey, a birthplace, or an invitation. Sometimes it is built slowly through memory, family, friendship and time. Sometimes it is complicated by the way other people see you. And sometimes a person belongs to several places at once.

Giannis's story makes that complexity visible. He is the son of Nigerian immigrants. He is Greek. He is an NBA player from Milwaukee. He is a brother who once shared basketball shoes. He is a father who has said he wants to raise his children in the city that became his second home. None of those identities makes the others less true.

Perhaps that is why the image of the young Giannis selling watches and glasses in Athens stays with me more than the image of him holding an NBA championship trophy. The trophy represents where his career took him. The street represents where his understanding of home began. Between those two places is an entire life. Athens did not disappear when Giannis moved to Milwaukee. Nigeria did not disappear because he grew up in Greece. And becoming an American NBA superstar did not require him to leave either identity behind. He carried them with him.

Maybe belonging is not finally finding the one place where you fit perfectly. Maybe it is realizing that home can have more than one name, and that you do not have to cut pieces of yourself away to make room for another.

Editor’s note: Sources: Giannis’s 60 Minutes interview with Steve Kroft, his 2022 ESPN interview, and news reports on his 2020 Bucks extension and his family’s 2021 citizenship ceremony.

Op-Ed · Writer’s Reflection

Looking Back

The poem was the hardest piece for me to write. I didn’t know how to start, and I had to keep checking that what I wrote down made sense to me. The piece I’m proudest of is my journalistic essay. I wrote it about Giannis Antetokounmpo, one of my favorite basketball players, and writing about someone I admire made the words easier to find.

If I could change one thing, I would deepen my vocabulary, so my next pieces can say what I mean in a stronger way. The biggest thing I learned is that I don’t write my best when I only have a short time. I need space to think and revise, so next time I’ll give myself that.
